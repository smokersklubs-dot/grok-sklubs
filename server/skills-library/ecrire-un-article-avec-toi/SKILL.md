# Write an article together

The person has things to say but no time to structure them. You draw out the material, then shape it with them. Their words and experience are the substance; you are the editor.

Ask once where to save (default: articles/<subject>/) and keep two files: **pile.md** (raw material) and **article.md** (the text). Re-read the file before each save: the person may have edited it. Never overwrite their changes.

## Stage 1: Fragments (explore, no structure yet)

Interview the person about the subject, one question at a time: what happened, what customers ask, what annoys them, what they would tell a friend. Every piece that might survive goes into pile.md, separated by a line with three dashes:
- a sharp sentence, a claim with its reason, an anecdote, a number, a customer quote, a half-thought "something about…";
- above all, a **key image**: one short phrase that names the whole idea (like "the empty shop window" for a website no one updates). If one comes up, mark it.

Add each fragment to the file as it comes, saying "I note that" in passing. No headings, no order. Stop when the person says the pile is big enough.

## Stage 2: The path (order for the reader)

1. Agree on what the reader **already knows** when they arrive (a shop customer? another baker?). Everything else must be explained before it is used.
2. Offer **2 or 3 possible starts**, each a different angle, from the pile.
3. After the choice, offer 2 or 3 possible **next steps** each time. A step can only use ideas already explained; note which new idea it introduces.
4. One step can be a sentence or a few paragraphs; if it needs subtitles, it is two steps.

## Stage 3: Writing (paragraph by paragraph)

1. Write the chosen opening into article.md, then ask "what does the reader need to hear now?" and pull from the pile to answer.
2. Write each agreed paragraph at once, so the article grows in front of them.
3. Push back on weak links: "What does this paragraph add?", "If we cut it, what breaks?", "The opening promised X, we drifted to Y."
4. If the pile lacks something ("we need an example here"), ask for it or cut the part. Never invent an anecdote, a figure or a quote.
5. Choose the form out loud: prose for an argument, a list for truly parallel items, a table when the same shape repeats three times or more.

The article ends when the journey is complete, not when the pile is empty: leftovers are normal. Then apply "Write like a human" for a last cleaning pass, and offer to turn it into a page (page_publish) or a newsletter draft.
