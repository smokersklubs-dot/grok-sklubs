# Customs and shipping abroad

For a shop that sends or buys goods across a customs border (outside the EU for a French shop, the UK, Switzerland, the US…). Work with the owner's real products and figures, in a file or spreadsheet, and give the source of every rule.

## 1. Classify each product (HS code)

The Harmonized System gives every product a 6-digit code (chapter 2 digits, heading 4, subheading 6); the EU adds digits (TARIC, 10), the US too (HTS, 10). Classification follows the General Rules of Interpretation **in order** — never rule 3 if rule 1 settles it:
1. **Rule 1**: the words of the headings and the section and chapter notes decide (most cases). Read the notes before going further.
2. **Rule 2**: an unfinished article is classified as the finished one if it already has its essential character; mixtures follow the material that gives that character.
3. **Rule 3**: if two headings fit, take (a) the most specific, else (b) the part giving the essential character (sets, composite goods), else (c) the last one in number order.
4. **Rule 4**: the most similar goods. **Rule 5**: cases and packing. **Rule 6**: the same logic at subheading level.
Look the code up in the official tool (EU: the TARIC database and its Access2Markets tool; UK: the online tariff; US: the HTS search). When in doubt on an important product, the owner can ask the customs service for a binding ruling (in the EU, a BTI/RTC). Write down the code, why, and the source.

## 2. The papers

- **Commercial invoice**: seller and buyer, precise description (not "gift" or "parts"), HS code, quantity, unit and total value, currency, country of origin, Incoterm, reason (sale, return, sample).
- **Parcels**: the carrier's customs form (CN22/CN23 by post) with the same description and value. Values must be true: undervaluing is a customs offence.
- **Origin**: a statement on origin when a trade agreement can lower duties (only if the product really qualifies; see 4).
- In the EU, the seller needs an EORI number to export or import; a shop selling to EU consumers from outside may use the import scheme for low-value goods (IOSS) for VAT. Check on the official customs site of the country.

## 3. Who pays what (Incoterms 2020)

The Incoterm says where the risk passes and who pays transport, duties and taxes. For a small shop: **DAP** (the buyer pays duties and VAT on arrival — warn them before they buy) or **DDP** (the shop pays everything: the buyer has no surprise, but you must be able to pay import VAT in their country). **EXW** pushes all the work to the buyer and is rarely right for exports. Write the chosen Incoterm on the invoice and on the shop's terms.

## 4. Preferential origin

A trade agreement (for example EU–UK, EU–Canada, EU–Japan) can bring duties to zero **only** if the product meets its origin rules (enough work or value from the zone, or a change of tariff heading). Check the product's own rule in the agreement, keep the proof (supplier declarations, bill of materials). Never claim a preference you cannot prove: the duties come back with a penalty.

## 5. Sanctions and restricted goods

Before a first shipment to a new customer or country, check the buyer against the official sanctions lists (EU consolidated list; UK, US OFAC lists for those destinations) and the country against embargoes; check if the product is "dual-use" or restricted (batteries, plants, cosmetics, food, alcohol, weapons-like items). A possible match: stop, tell the owner, do not ship until it is cleared. Keep a note of each check (date, list, result).

## 6. A letter from customs

Read what is asked and by when; gather the invoice, proof of origin and payment; answer only what is asked, with documents; if an error was made, a voluntary correction before an inspection is usually treated better. For anything with a penalty, suggest the owner call a customs broker.

## What needs the owner

Choosing the Incoterm and the prices shown, any claim of preferential origin, any shipment with a possible sanctions match, every answer sent to customs.
